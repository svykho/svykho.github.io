A scalar field first

Separate a wave equation on a black hole into multipoles, and each one becomes a wave on a line meeting a single potential barrier.

Why a scalar first

Gravitational waves are the real goal, but they come with indices, gauge freedom and long algebra (Lesson 4). A scalar field, a single number at each point of spacetime, has none of that. Its simplest version, massless and not interacting with anything, obeys the wave equation of the curved spacetime around the hole,

$$\Box\Phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\partial_\mu\!\left(\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\nu\Phi\right) = 0.$$

It is a toy, but an honest one. Everything we find in this lesson, the potential barrier, the scattering, the ringing and the tail, carries over to gravitational waves with only the shape of the barrier changed.

Separating the variables

Schwarzschild is spherically symmetric, so the angles can be split off with spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)$, the same functions that describe the shapes of atomic orbitals. Write

$$\Phi(t, r, \theta, \phi) = \sum_{\ell, m} \frac{\psi_{\ell m}(t, r)}{r}\, Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi).$$

The factor $1/r$ is there because a wave spreading over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$ must fall off as $1/r$ to carry a constant energy. Each $\psi_{\ell m}$ then obeys an equation of its own, the same for every $m$:

$$\left[-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial r_*^2} - V_\ell(r)\right]\psi = 0, \qquad V_\ell(r) = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2} + \frac{2M}{r^3}\right].$$

A wave on a line, the tortoise coordinate $r_*$ of Lesson 2, with a potential. The problem in four dimensions has become a family of problems in two.

The full calculation

For the Schwarzschild metric $\sqrt{-g} = r^2\sin\theta$, and the wave operator is

$$\Box\Phi = -\frac{1}{f}\,\partial_t^2\Phi + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\partial_r\!\left(r^2 f\,\partial_r\Phi\right) + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\nabla^2_\Omega\Phi,$$

where $\nabla^2_\Omega$ is the Laplacian on the unit sphere, with $\nabla^2_\Omega Y_{\ell m} = -\ell(\ell+1)\,Y_{\ell m}$. Put in $\Phi = (\psi/r)\,Y_{\ell m}$. The radial term gives

$$\partial_r\!\left(r^2 f\,\partial_r\frac{\psi}{r}\right) = \partial_r\left(r f \psi' - f\psi\right) = r\,(f\psi')' - f'\psi.$$

Multiply the whole equation by $r f$ and use $f\,\partial_r = \partial_{r_*}$, so that $f\,(f\psi')' = \partial^2_{r_*}\psi$:

$$-\partial_t^2\psi + \partial^2_{r_*}\psi - f\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2} + \frac{f'}{r}\right]\psi = 0.$$

With $f' = 2M/r^2$ this is the equation above. Fields of other spins give the same structure with $2M/r^3$ replaced by $(1 - s^2)\,2M/r^3$: $s = 1$ for the electromagnetic field and $s = 2$ for axial gravitational waves, which is the Regge–Wheeler potential of Lesson 4.

The barrier

The potential is the whole story, so it is worth reading carefully.

  • At the horizon, $r_* \to -\infty$, it vanishes because of the factor $f$. Waves there move freely, into the hole.
  • Far away, $r_* \to +\infty$, it falls off as $\ell(\ell+1)/r^2$, the centrifugal barrier familiar from the hydrogen atom. Waves there move freely, out to infinity.
  • In between it has a single peak. For $\ell = 0$ the peak is at $r = 8M/3$; as $\ell$ grows it moves towards $r = 3M$, the light ring of Lesson 2, and its height grows like $\ell(\ell+1)/27M^2$.

The peak at the light ring is no accident. A wave with large $\ell$ behaves like a beam of light with a large angular momentum, and the barrier is the effective potential of the light rays of Lesson 2, multiplied by $\ell^2$.

A wave with $\omega^2$ well below the top of the barrier is mostly reflected; one well above it mostly passes through into the hole. In between, part of it tunnels through. Try it.

What the figure shows

Three things are worth noticing.

  • The barrier filters frequencies. For $\ell = 1$ the top is at $\sqrt{V_0} \approx 0.31/M$. A packet centred on $M\omega = 0.2$ is mostly reflected; one centred on $M\omega = 0.5$ goes almost entirely into the hole. A packet contains a spread of frequencies, so the change is gradual. Higher $\ell$ raises the bar.
  • Energy is conserved. What is reflected plus what is transmitted adds up to what came in, as the numbers on the right show. The horizon absorbs, but the equation outside it loses nothing.
  • The barrier rings. Look at the reflected packet just after it leaves the barrier: it has a short tail of oscillations that were not in the original packet. That is the black hole ringing, at frequencies of its own. The next lessons are about those oscillations.
Key equation
$$V_\ell(r) = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2} + \frac{2M}{r^3}\right]$$

The barrier seen by each multipole of a massless scalar field. It vanishes at the horizon and at infinity and peaks near the light ring.

In one sentence

Spherical harmonics turn a wave on a black hole into a wave on a line with a potential barrier near the light ring, which reflects low frequencies, transmits high ones and rings in between.

Exercise

(a) Find the position and the height of the peak of the $\ell = 0$ scalar potential. (b) The exact fundamental quasinormal mode of this field is $M\omega = 0.1105 - 0.1049\,i$. How well does $\sqrt{V_0}$ estimate its frequency, and what is its quality factor $Q = \operatorname{Re}\omega/2|\operatorname{Im}\omega|$? (c) Show that for large $\ell$ the peak tends to $r = 3M$ and its height to $\ell(\ell+1)/27M^2$.

Show the solution

(a) With $M = 1$ and $\ell = 0$, $V = 2/r^3 - 4/r^4$, so $V' = -6/r^4 + 16/r^5 = 0$ at $r = 8/3$. There

$$V_0 = \frac{2 \cdot 27}{512} - \frac{4 \cdot 81}{4096} = \frac{27}{1024} \approx 0.0264\,M^{-2}.$$

(b) $\sqrt{V_0} \approx 0.162$, almost 50 % above the exact $0.1105$. The mode decays almost as fast as it oscillates, $Q \approx 0.53$: it is barely a ringing at all. Barrier-top estimates work for sharp, tall barriers and long-lived modes, which means large $\ell$.

(c) For large $\ell$ the term $2M/r^3$ is negligible and $V \approx \ell(\ell+1)\,f/r^2$, whose maximum is at $r = 3M$ with value $\ell(\ell+1)/27M^2$, as for light in Lesson 2.
