Lesson 06 · Part II · Ringing black holes

Quasinormal modes

A black hole rings at a ladder of complex frequencies set only by its mass. What they are, how to compute them, and how to hear them.

The definition

Lesson 4 left us with one equation for each multipole,

$$\frac{d^2\Psi}{dr_*^2} + \left[\omega^2 - V(r)\right]\Psi = 0,$$

and Lesson 1 told us what to ask of it. A quasinormal mode is a solution that is purely ingoing at the horizon and purely outgoing at infinity:

$$\Psi \propto e^{-i\omega r_*} \quad (r_* \to -\infty), \qquad \Psi \propto e^{+i\omega r_*} \quad (r_* \to +\infty).$$

With the time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, the first is a wave moving into the hole and the second a wave moving away from it. Nothing comes in from outside, and nothing comes out of the horizon. Only a discrete set of complex frequencies $\omega_{\ell n}$ satisfies both conditions. For each $\ell$ they are labelled by the overtone number $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, in order of increasing damping, and each has a mirror partner $-\omega_{\ell n}^*$, the same mode turning the other way.

The frequencies depend on nothing but $\ell$, $n$ and the mass. Since $M$ is the only scale in the problem, $\omega \propto 1/M$, and we quote the dimensionless number $M\omega$. Axial and polar perturbations share the same spectrum (Lesson 4), so there is a single list.

The spectrum

Here it is, for the first three multipoles and eight overtones of each, computed to many digits with the method described further down.

What the spectrum says

  • The pitch grows with $\ell$, roughly as $\operatorname{Re} M\omega \approx (\ell + \tfrac12)/\sqrt{27}$.
  • The fundamental modes of all multipoles decay at almost the same rate, $\operatorname{Im} M\omega \approx -0.09$. The amplitude drops by a factor $e$ in about $11M$.
  • Overtones die fast. The first overtone of $\ell = 2$ decays three times faster than the fundamental, the second more than five times faster.
  • Down the ladder the decay rate grows in steps that approach $1/4M$, while the pitch slowly drops. For very high overtones the pitch tends to $\ln 3/(8\pi M) \approx 0.044/M$, a limit found numerically by Nollert and derived by Motl.

Why the light ring

Where does $(\ell + \tfrac12)/\sqrt{27}$ come from? Think of a wave with large $\ell$ as a beam of light. Lesson 4 showed that the potential peaks near $r = 3M$, the light ring, where light can circle the hole. That orbit is unstable: a ray that starts slightly off it spirals away, its distance from the orbit growing like $e^{\lambda t}$. For large $\ell$ a quasinormal mode is a wave trapped on the light ring and slowly leaking off it, and

$$\omega \approx \Omega_c\left(\ell + \tfrac12\right) - i\lambda\left(n + \tfrac12\right),$$

where $\Omega_c$ is the angular frequency of the circular light orbit and $\lambda$ its instability rate. For Schwarzschild both are equal to $1/(3\sqrt3\,M) = 1/(\sqrt{27}\,M)$. The pitch is the orbital frequency times the number of wavelengths that fit around the orbit; the damping is the rate at which the orbit sheds light. This picture (Cardoso and collaborators, 2009) holds well beyond Schwarzschild, and it ties the ringdown to the black-hole shadow seen by the Event Horizon Telescope, whose size is also set by the light ring.

Quick approximations

The light-ring formula is only the first term of an expansion in large $\ell$, and for $\ell = 2$ it misses the pitch by almost 30 %. Two other quick estimates use the shape of the barrier near its top, where it looks like an upside-down parabola. Call $V_0$ its height and $V_0''$ its curvature in $r_*$.

  • Pöschl–Teller. Replace the barrier by the solvable bump of Lesson 1 with the same height and curvature, $\alpha^2 = -V_0''/2V_0$, and use its exact modes (Ferrari and Mashhoon, 1984).
  • WKB. Match WKB solutions on either side of the barrier through its parabolic top. At first order this gives $\omega^2 = V_0 - i\left(n + \tfrac12\right)\sqrt{-2V_0''}$ (Schutz and Will, 1985). Higher orders (Iyer and Will, 1987, and later work) are much more accurate.

Leaver's method

In 1985 Edward Leaver found a way to compute quasinormal frequencies to any precision. The idea is to build the boundary conditions into the solution from the start and expand what remains in a power series:

$$\Psi = (r - 2M)^{-2iM\omega}\, r^{4iM\omega}\, e^{i\omega(r - 2M)} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k \left(\frac{r - 2M}{r}\right)^{k}.$$

The prefactor is ingoing at the horizon and outgoing at infinity. The coefficients $a_k$ obey a three-term recurrence, and the series converges all the way out to infinity only when $\omega$ is a quasinormal frequency. That condition can be written as the vanishing of a continued fraction, a function of $\omega$ whose zeros any root finder can locate.

The full calculation

Work in units with $2M = 1$, so that the frequencies come out as $2M\omega$, and write $\rho = -i\omega$. Putting the series into the wave equation gives

$$\alpha_k\, a_{k+1} + \beta_k\, a_k + \gamma_k\, a_{k-1} = 0, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots,$$

with $a_{-1} = 0$ and, for a field of spin $s$ and $\varepsilon = s^2 - 1$,

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha_k &= k^2 + (2\rho + 2)\,k + 2\rho + 1, \\ \beta_k &= -\left[2k^2 + (8\rho + 2)\,k + 8\rho^2 + 4\rho + \ell(\ell+1) - \varepsilon\right], \\ \gamma_k &= k^2 + 4\rho\, k + 4\rho^2 - \varepsilon - 1. \end{aligned}$$

A three-term recurrence has two independent solutions. The one we need is the minimal solution, which decays fastest as $k \to \infty$ and makes the series converge at infinity. It exists exactly when

$$0 = \beta_0 - \cfrac{\alpha_0\,\gamma_1}{\beta_1 - \cfrac{\alpha_1\,\gamma_2}{\beta_2 - \cfrac{\alpha_2\,\gamma_3}{\beta_3 - \cdots}}}$$

For the overtone $n$ it is much better to invert the fraction $n$ times, which makes the wanted root the most stable one (Leaver, 1985). For this course we truncated the fraction at depth 4000, found the roots with a Newton-type solver working with 30 digits, and divided by two to get $M\omega$. The whole computation is about thirty lines of Python, given and explained in Lesson 12, and it agrees with the standard tables to every digit shown here.

Measure it yourself

There is a third way to find the modes, the one Nature uses: hit the black hole and listen. The figure below solves the Regge–Wheeler equation (or the Zerilli equation, or those for a scalar or electromagnetic field) in time, in your browser, for a wave packet thrown at the hole, and records the wave far away. Then it fits a single damped sinusoid to a stretch of the signal $40M$ long, starting where you choose.

Too early, just right, too late

The lower panel tells the story of a ringdown in a single curve.

  • Too early, near the peak, the fit is poor. The signal still contains the direct response to the packet and the overtones, which die quickly but are loud at first.
  • In the middle there is a plateau where the fit agrees with Leaver's value to about one part in a thousand or better. Here the signal is a single quasinormal mode.
  • Too late, the agreement gets worse again. The ringing has decayed below a slower signal that does not oscillate, the late-time tail, which falls off as a power of time rather than exponentially (Lesson 8).

The packet oscillates, at frequency $0.4/M$. A smooth bump would also make the hole ring, but an oscillating packet carries little low-frequency content, and that keeps the tail weak. This effect explains why tails are so faint in many merger signals (León Vega and collaborators, 2026). Try the axial and polar perturbations with the same $\ell$: the signals look different, but the fitted frequencies agree, as Chandrasekhar's argument in Lesson 4 says they must.

How loudly each mode rings depends on the packet, not only on the hole: the black hole acts as a filter that keeps the part of the perturbation that overlaps with its spectrum (Svyatkovskyy Kholyavka and collaborators, 2026). That is the subject of Lesson 7.

From M = 1 to hertz

To turn $M\omega$ into something a detector measures, put back $G$ and $c$. The unit of time is $GM/c^3$, which is $4.93\ \mu\mathrm{s}$ for one solar mass, so

$$f = \frac{\operatorname{Re}(M\omega)}{2\pi}\,\frac{c^3}{GM}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{|\operatorname{Im}\,\omega|} = \frac{GM/c^3}{|\operatorname{Im}(M\omega)|}.$$

For a black hole of ten solar masses the fundamental $\ell = 2$ mode rings at $1.21$ kHz and decays in $0.55$ ms. Both scale with the mass: a black hole a million times heavier rings a million times lower, for a million times longer.

Key equation
$$M\omega_{\ell n} \approx \frac{\ell + \frac12}{\sqrt{27}} - i\,\frac{n + \frac12}{\sqrt{27}}$$

The light-ring estimate of the quasinormal frequencies of a Schwarzschild black hole. The exact fundamental mode of $\ell = 2$ is $M\omega = 0.3737 - 0.0890\,i$.

In one sentence

A Schwarzschild black hole rings at a discrete ladder of complex frequencies set only by its mass, the notes of waves trapped at the light ring, which Leaver's method computes exactly and which can be heard in any signal the hole emits.

Exercise

A ringdown is measured with frequency $f = 250$ Hz and damping time $\tau = 4$ ms, and assumed to be the fundamental $\ell = 2$ mode. (a) If the black hole did not spin, what mass would each of the two numbers give? (b) Are they consistent? (c) Compute the quality factor $Q = \pi f \tau$ and compare it with that of a non-spinning black hole.

Show the solution

(a) For $10\,M_\odot$ the mode has $f = 1207.4$ Hz and $\tau = 0.554$ ms, and $f \propto 1/M$, $\tau \propto M$. The frequency gives $M = 10 \times 1207.5/250 \approx 48\,M_\odot$; the damping time gives $M = 10 \times 4/0.554 \approx 72\,M_\odot$.

(b) No.

(c) $Q = \pi \times 250 \times 0.004 \approx 3.1$. For a non-spinning black hole, whatever its mass,

$$Q = \frac{\operatorname{Re}\omega}{2\,|\operatorname{Im}\omega|} = \frac{0.37367}{0.17792} \approx 2.10 .$$

The quality factor depends only on the shape of the spectrum, not on its scale, so a measured $Q$ above 2.1 says that the black hole cannot be a Schwarzschild one. It is spinning: spin makes the dominant mode ring longer. Measuring two numbers of one mode gives both the mass and the spin. That is black-hole spectroscopy, and Lesson 9 adds the spin.
